Every call with my parents gets to the same question.

"How's work going?"

They know the outline. They know I'm a software engineer. They know I write code, that I work at a startup, that some weeks are heavier than others.

What they don't have is the inside of it. What the code actually does. Why one problem can take a whole week. Why I sound the way I do on the day something finally works.

So when they ask, I try to give them the inside. It takes about ten minutes. It wanders into things neither of them has any reason to know.

They stay on the line for all of it.

I've been thinking about those ten minutes a lot.

## A family of accountants

Both my parents are chartered accountants. Both of them have worked corporate jobs their entire working lives.

My elder brother is a chartered accountant too.

In a house like that, there is a path already laid out for you. Nobody has to say it out loud. It's just sitting there, the way the furniture is sitting there. And it wasn't a theory, either. I had watched someone a few years ahead of me walk it, and watched it work.

I was eleven when I started unscrewing things instead.

There was one computer in the house I'd been told not to open, because it would break. I opened it. Then I spent my summer vacations building apps nobody had asked for and putting them on the Play Store. Then, in the year I was supposed to be preparing for entrance exams, I wired a Raspberry Pi into my room's switchboard so I wouldn't have to get up to turn off the light. The first time it worked, the wrong thing switched on and my mom walked out of the room.

From the other side of that door, it can't have looked like a plan. It must have looked like a kid spending his most important year on a light switch.

They let me.

And when the time came to actually choose, I chose engineering. The one person at the table who wasn't going into finance. I don't remember an argument. I don't remember a speech about what I was giving up, either. I remember that it was my decision, and that they treated it like one.

## Nothing I did looked like their careers

In my first year of college I joined a startup. A handful of people building a fantasy football app, and me, the second engineer, working on it between classes.

Then I started writing code for an open source project where my colleagues were usernames in other time zones. People I had never met, at an organization with no office I went to. At nineteen I got on a flight to Bangkok to meet them.

Then there were the weekends. Hackathons where I didn't sleep, came back wrecked, and sometimes came back having won. A college committee I ended up running, thirty-six people, putting on a hackathon of our own.

And now I'm a founding engineer at another startup.

They knew about every one of these. I told them each time. A startup. Open source. A hackathon this weekend, I'll be back Sunday night.

What they couldn't know, because nobody can from the outside, was whether any of it was a good idea.

Their own careers were built on things you can point to. A company people have heard of. An office. A designation that means the same thing next year as it does this year. A ladder where you can see the next rung. The things I chose were smaller and newer than that, and you couldn't see very far ahead on any of them.

They backed every one of those choices anyway.

## Signing without the papers

For years I filed this under "my parents are relaxed about things." I don't think that's what it was.

Think about what a chartered accountant actually does. You don't take someone's word for a number. You ask for the papers. You trace it back to where it came from, and only when it holds up do you put your name under it. My parents have done exactly that for decades.

Then their younger son shows up with a life they have no way to check. No papers to read. Nothing to trace. Just him, saying it's going well.

And they put their names under it. Again and again, for years.

Backing a choice you understand is easy. You can see for yourself whether it's a good one. Backing a choice you _can't_ check is something else. There's nothing to hold it up against. All you have is the person.

That's what they have been going on this whole time. Not the work. Me.

## The floor

It took me even longer to see the other half.

I get to join small teams early, when there are five people and a whiteboard. I get to pick the work I can't stop thinking about over the work that looks safest on paper. I've done that again and again without ever really feeling the drop underneath.

That isn't because I'm brave. It's because two people did the steady thing, every month, for as long as I've been alive. The risk I take was never free. Somebody paid for it in advance.

I used to think of my path as the opposite of theirs. Startups against corporate. Small and new against big and established.

It isn't the opposite. Mine is standing on theirs.

## I ended up building books

Here's the part that makes me laugh.

I went as far from accounting as I thought it was possible to go. And if you look at what I work on now, underneath all the vocabulary, it is this: two sides of a trade that have to settle together. Balances that have to reconcile. A record of who owns what that everyone can check and nobody can quietly change.

It's a ledger. I build ledgers.

Three chartered accountants in the family, and the one who got away keeps books for a living. We might be less different than I thought.

I don't think that's the only thing I took from them, either. I'm the one who can't leave a number that's slightly off. Who doesn't like putting my name on something I haven't checked myself. Who gets uneasy when a thing works but I can't say why. I always assumed that was just how I was wired.

I think I know where the wiring came from.

## Ten minutes

Back to the phone call.

For a long time I thought the long explanation was failing. I'd get to the end, they'd say it sounds good, and I'd hear that as the point where I lost them.

I had it backwards.

Two people who have their own long days, their own work, their own things to worry about, ask me a question and then give me ten minutes of their evening to answer it. On a subject they will never need. In words they didn't grow up with. They don't hurry me. They don't change the subject.

That isn't them not understanding. That's about as understood as I ever get.

And I don't think "how's work going" was ever really about work. They're asking if I'm okay. If I'm tired. If the thing I chose is still the thing I'd choose. It all fits inside those three words.

The answer they're listening for isn't in what I say. It's in how I sound while I'm saying it. They can't follow the code. They can hear their son, and they have always been able to tell the difference between him doing fine and him saying he's fine.

## If you two are reading this

I don't say this enough, so I'm saying it here, where I had time to get the words right.

Thank you for not stopping the kid with the screwdriver, even after you'd told him not to.

Thank you for the exam year, when you had every reason to take the Raspberry Pi away and didn't.

Thank you for treating my decisions as mine, long before I had done anything to earn that.

Thank you for asking, every call. And for listening to the long version when the short one would have been easier for all of us.

Work is going well. Yes, I'm eating.

My parents know I write code. They couldn't tell you much more about the work than that.

They could tell you everything about the person doing it.
